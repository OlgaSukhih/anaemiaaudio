Introduction

Today we are going to compare several types of anaemia. Anaemia means that the haemoglobin concentration is below the appropriate normal range. Haemoglobin carries oxygen in red blood cells. Patients may describe tiredness, dizziness or breathlessness. These symptoms suggest a problem, but they do not identify its cause. A full blood count helps us examine haemoglobin and blood cell counts. Further tests and the patient’s history help us build a differential diagnosis. Think about three broad mechanisms: blood loss, reduced production, and increased destruction of red cells.

Iron-deficiency anaemia

First, consider iron-deficiency anaemia. The body needs iron to make haemoglobin. If iron stores become depleted, haemoglobin production suffers. The red cells are often smaller than normal. Low ferritin supports depleted iron stores. A diet deficient in iron is one possible explanation, but it is not the only one. Heavy menstrual bleeding or chronic gastrointestinal blood loss may also cause iron deficiency. An apparently adequate diet does not exclude these possibilities. Some patients lose small amounts of blood without noticing visible bleeding. Once iron deficiency is identified, we still need to investigate why it developed.

Vitamin B12 deficiency and pernicious anaemia

Vitamin B12 deficiency is different. It can interfere with healthy blood cell production, and red cells may be larger than normal. Patients may have anaemia, but they can also have neurological symptoms, such as tingling or difficulty walking. Vitamin B12 deficiency can occur without anaemia. Pernicious anaemia is a specific cause of vitamin B12 deficiency, not a name for every case. It involves an autoimmune problem affecting intrinsic factor, which helps the body absorb vitamin B12. A person may eat enough vitamin B12 and still fail to absorb it properly. Therefore, an adequate diet cannot exclude pernicious anaemia. Doctors investigate the cause using the history and additional tests.

Haemolytic anaemia

In haemolytic anaemia, red blood cells are destroyed faster than they can be replaced. The main problem is increased destruction, rather than simply insufficient iron intake. Patients may develop jaundice, meaning yellowing of the skin or eyes, and dark urine. The bone marrow often responds by producing more young red cells, called reticulocytes. Different inherited or acquired conditions can cause haemolysis. Recognising red cell destruction does not automatically establish its exact cause. We need further evidence before deciding which condition is responsible.

Aplastic anaemia

Aplastic anaemia involves failure of the bone marrow to produce enough blood cells. Unlike isolated anaemia, it may affect white blood cells and platelets as well as red cells. Low red cell production can contribute to tiredness. Low white blood cell counts can increase susceptibility to infection, while low platelets can contribute to bruising and bleeding. However, several low blood cell counts do not prove aplastic anaemia. Other bone marrow disorders must be included in the differential diagnosis. Specialist assessment and bone marrow examination help distinguish these possibilities.

Putting the evidence together

When discussing a patient, separate the type of anaemia from its underlying cause. Iron-deficiency anaemia may be clear, while the source of blood loss remains unknown. Vitamin B12 deficiency does not automatically mean pernicious anaemia. Jaundice can be a useful clue, but it is not a diagnosis by itself. Ask what the results support, what they argue against, and what they cannot exclude. Your most likely diagnosis should fit the overall pattern. A useful conclusion explains both the leading possibility and the information still needed.

Pernicious anaemia is one specific cause of vitamin B12 deficiency. An autoimmune problem affects intrinsic factor and absorption. Other causes of B12 deficiency also exist.

3. Compare haemolytic and aplastic anaemia in two sentences.

Haemolytic anaemia mainly involves increased destruction of red cells. Aplastic anaemia involves reduced production in the bone marrow and may affect several blood cell types.
